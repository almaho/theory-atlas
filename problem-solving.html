RELAX THE APPROXIMATION

Dynamic matching

START HERE

Can you maintain a 2-approximation?

Maintain a maximal matching: every edge touches a matched vertex. On an insertion, add the edge if both endpoints are free. On deletion of a matched edge, scan the two newly free endpoints for free neighbors.

Try it. Prove that maximality gives |M| ≥ OPT/2. Then identify the work hidden inside “find a free neighbor.”

Hint and proof

Charge each edge of an optimum matching to an incident edge of M. Maximality ensures that an incident edge exists, and each edge of M receives at most two charges.

With adjacency lists and constant-time matched-status tests, a simple repair scans O(Δ) neighbors per update, where Δ bounds the maximum degree over the sequence. This is a baseline algorithm; the approximation proof alone does not give a faster update time.

What this instance teaches

After insertion, OPT = k + 1 while the old matching has k edges. It remains valid exactly when 1/k ≤ ε. With k = 5, ε = 0.2 permits no repair; ε = 0.1 requires a matching of 6 edges.

On this path the perfect matching is unique. Obtaining it from the displayed matching removes k edges and inserts k + 1, changing 2k + 1 memberships. FIND THE CERTIFICATE

How far must a local improvement look?

Suppose M has no augmenting path of length at most 2r − 1. Move r to connect a local condition to a global approximation.

Try to prove the guarantee

In the symmetric difference of M with an optimum matching, every augmenting component contains at least r edges of M. Each contributes just one extra optimum edge. Charge that extra edge across those r matched edges to obtain OPT ≤ (1 + 1/r)|M|.

The path with 2r + 1 edges and its r alternating internal edges attains this ratio. For a (1 + ε) guarantee, take r ≥ 1/ε. Maintaining this certificate after every update is a separate algorithmic problem.

SPEND THE APPROXIMATION SLACK

When can you postpone rebuilding?

Start with an exact matching of q edges. After t updates, retain only its surviving edges. A single insertion increases OPT by at most one, and a deletion removes at most one selected edge.

Try it. For q = 100 and ε = 0.1, how many arbitrary updates can you tolerate before this simple guarantee runs out?

Hint and worked answer

Write a + d = t for insertion and deletion counts. Then OPT ≤ q + a and |M| ≥ q − d. For t < q, the ratio is at most (q + a)/(q − d) ≤ q/(q − t).

Thus t ≤ εq/(1 + ε) suffices for a (1 + ε)-approximation: nine updates in this example. Ten are not certified by this bound; that does not mean every ten-update sequence fails.

The exact starting solution is an exercise assumption. Real rebuilding algorithms allocate error to both the initial approximate solution and later drift. Rebuild cost and the distinction between amortized and worst-case update time still need analysis.

Technique: Gupta–Peng, Section 3.

Reusable tool

Relax the target until a simple invariant works. Tighten it only after identifying the obstruction and the cost of repairing it.

Foundations: CMU matching lecture notes · Gupta and Peng on fully dynamic approximate matching. Continue in Atlas: dynamic matching bounds · inside the ORS algorithm.

CHANGE THE OBJECTIVE

Weighted matching

Which parts of the unweighted proof survive when an edge can be worth much more than its neighbors?

Three edges are enough to break the intuition.

A four-vertex path has weights 1, W, 1. The two outer edges maximize cardinality. The middle edge may maximize weight.

Try it. Increase W. Can cardinality alone guarantee any constant approximation to weight? Then set W just above 1: what happens to descending-weight greedy?

Worked answer

The outer matching weighs 2; the optimum weighs max(2, W). Thus a cardinality optimum can be arbitrarily poor for weight as W grows.

For 1 < W < 2, descending-weight greedy takes the middle edge and loses to the outer pair. Its ratio is 2/W, approaching 2 as W approaches 1 from above. For nonnegative weights, the general greedy 2-approximation follows by charging each optimum edge to an earlier, at-least-as-heavy selected edge; at most two optimum edges charge one selected edge.

Reusable tool

Re-run a proof under the new objective. Cardinality charges count edges; weighted charges must also compare their values.

Technique reference: Drake and Hougardy on weighted matching approximation, including the descending-weight greedy baseline. The three-edge instance is a worked exercise. Weighted matching in Atlas.

TEST A TRANSFERRED INVARIANT

Maximal independent set

Maximality gives a constant approximation for matching. Does that same idea work for independent set?

A star exposes the difference.

Choose the center or all leaves. Both are independent sets that cannot be enlarged.

Try it. Find the failed step in the matching charging proof. Which quantity replaces “at most two”?

Worked answer

The center alone is maximal and has size 1. All d leaves form a maximum independent set of size d. The ratio d is unbounded with degree.

For a general graph of maximum degree Δ, assign every vertex of an optimum independent set to a selected vertex in its closed neighborhood. A selected vertex can receive at most Δ + 1 assignments, giving the coarse degree-dependent guarantee. The star shows why no degree-independent constant follows from maximality alone.

Reusable tool

Transfer the proof, not just the slogan. Check the congestion of its charging map. Atlas studies maximal MIS as a feasibility task; that is different from maximizing its size.

Continue with a real dynamic technique: Behnezhad, Derakhshan, Hajiaghayi, Stein and Sudan on fully dynamic maximal independent set. The star here is an elementary counterexample about the objective, not a runtime analysis of that algorithm. Dynamic MIS in Atlas.

BUY A LOCAL REPAIR WITH SLACK

Vertex coloring

A little extra room can turn a global requirement into a local operation.

Insert an edge between equal colors.

Fix a maximum-degree bound Δ for the update sequence and use Δ + 1 colors. If an inserted edge creates a conflict, consider recoloring just one endpoint.

uno edgev

The two vertices may share a color while they are nonadjacent.

Try it. Why does u always have an available color after insertion? What work is needed to find it?

Worked answer

At most Δ neighbors forbid at most Δ colors. Among Δ + 1 choices, one remains. Recoloring u to that color fixes all of u’s incident edges and changes no other edge constraint.

Scanning the neighbors and marking their colors costs O(Δ) in a simple adjacency-list implementation, even though only one vertex is recolored. The tiny demonstration shows the conflict and repair; the palette argument applies to arbitrary neighborhoods under the fixed degree bound.

Reusable tool

Separate the number of changed outputs from the work needed to discover a valid change.

Reference: Bhattacharya, Chakrabarty, Henzinger and Nanongkai on dynamic graph coloring. The local example illustrates the elementary Δ + 1 palette argument; advanced update bounds require additional machinery. Vertex coloring in Atlas.

LOOK FOR AN EXTENSION OBSTRUCTION

Edge coloring

A graph may have a small coloring even when the current partial coloring cannot be extended locally.

Two colors exist. Neither is free.

A path has a red left edge and a green right edge. Insert the middle edge. Its left endpoint forbids red; its right endpoint forbids green.

Try it. Explain why Δ = 2 does not make the present partial coloring extendible with two colors. Generalize the number of colors forbidden to one edge.

Worked answer

Using a third color extends the existing assignment. Alternatively, recolor the right outer edge red and then color the middle edge green: the whole path uses two colors, but the repair changes an existing color.

In a simple graph of maximum degree Δ, an edge is adjacent to at most 2Δ − 2 other edges. A palette of 2Δ − 1 colors therefore guarantees a greedy choice. This is a local extension guarantee, not a claim that so many colors are necessary for the graph.

Reusable tool

Distinguish existence from extendibility. Extra colors can substitute for recoloring; a tighter palette needs a more capable repair.

Reference: Dynamic graph coloring, Appendix A, for the 2Δ − 1 edge-coloring baseline. The path is a worked extension obstruction. Greedy edge coloring in Atlas.

FIND A SMALL CERTIFICATE OF COST

Correlation clustering

Positive edges want their endpoints together; negative edges want them apart. Which local pattern makes some disagreement unavoidable?

The inconsistent triangle

In this complete, unweighted signed graph, ab and bc are positive; ac is negative. Try three partitions.

Try it. Prove every partition pays at least one disagreement. Can you add this lower bound across all bad triangles in a larger graph?

Worked answer

Satisfying both positive edges places a, b and c in the same cluster, violating ac. Separating a from c instead cuts at least one positive edge. OPT is 1, attained by the all-together partition or either positive pair plus a singleton.

Counting every bad triangle independently can overcount: one disagreeing edge can belong to many triangles. Edge-disjoint bad triangles give a valid additive lower bound. More general fractional packings need an edge-capacity constraint.

Reusable tool

Find the smallest unavoidable obstruction, then control overlap before using many copies as a lower bound or charging argument.

Technique reference: Ailon, Charikar and Newman on correlation clustering, especially bad triangles and randomized Pivot on complete signed graphs. The example proves OPT for one triangle; it does not establish Pivot’s expected approximation guarantee. Correlation clustering in Atlas.
